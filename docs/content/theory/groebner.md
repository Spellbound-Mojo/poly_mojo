# Gröbner bases

Fix a field $K$ ($\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb Q(i)$ or $\mathbb F_p$), variables
$x_1 > \cdots > x_n$, and a monomial order $\prec$ (Definition R-2).

**Definition B-1.** For an ideal $I \subseteq K[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, a
finite $G = \{g_1, \dots, g_t\} \subseteq I$ is a *Gröbner basis* of $I$
when the leading monomials of $G$ generate the ideal of leading monomials
of $I$: $\langle \operatorname{LM}(G) \rangle = \langle \operatorname{LM}(I) \rangle$.
The *S-polynomial* of $f$ and $g$ is
$S(f, g) = \frac{L}{\operatorname{LT}(f)} f - \frac{L}{\operatorname{LT}(g)} g$
with $L = \operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{LM} f, \operatorname{LM} g)$.

**Theorem B-2 (Buchberger's criterion, cited).** $G$ is a Gröbner basis
of $\langle G \rangle$ if and only if every S-polynomial $S(g_i, g_j)$ has
remainder $0$ on division by $G$ (Theorem V-4) [Buchberger 1965; Cox,
Little and O'Shea 2015, ch. 2, §6, Theorem 6].

**Theorem B-3 (Buchberger's algorithm, cited).** Starting from the input
generators, repeatedly adding nonzero remainders of S-polynomials
terminates (by the ascending chain condition on monomial ideals, Dickson's
lemma) with a Gröbner basis of the ideal generated by the inputs. The
product and chain criteria of Gebauer and Möller skip S-pairs whose
remainders are known to be $0$, without affecting the result [Gebauer and
Möller 1988; Cox, Little and O'Shea 2015, ch. 2, §§7–10].

**Scheduling and reduction.** Pair selection uses a binary heap ordered
by LCM under the chosen monomial order, then by the two stable basis indices.
This is exactly the earlier linear selector's total order. Installation applies
the same pair criteria before rebuilding the heap, so its representation does
not alter which S-polynomial is selected next. Basis polynomials are immutable
after insertion; their leading monomials and leading-coefficient inverses can
therefore be retained by index. The active reducer order is sorted after an
insertion and reused until the next insertion. Initial generator reductions and
cofactor propagation retain their established schedules.

**F4.** Over a prime field below $2^{31}$, under GRLEX and GREVLEX and
without cofactor tracking, the library runs F4 [Faugère 1999] instead of
reducing one S-polynomial at a time. Each round takes every pending pair whose
LCM has the least degree, writes both halves of each S-polynomial as rows of a
sparse matrix, adds a row $m \cdot g$ for every monomial of the matrix that a
leading monomial divides (symbolic preprocessing), and row-reduces the matrix.
A reduced row whose leading monomial no other row has is a polynomial of the
ideal whose leading monomial no basis element divides, so it joins the basis.
Pairs are pruned by the same Gebauer–Möller criteria, and when no pairs remain
every S-polynomial has a standard representation in terms of the basis, which
is Buchberger's criterion. The final interreduction is one more matrix. The
reduced basis is unique (Theorem B-4), so F4 returns exactly the basis
Buchberger's algorithm returns.

**Theorem B-4 (reduced bases, cited).** Every ideal has exactly one
reduced Gröbner basis for a given order: monic, and with no monomial of
any element divisible by the leading monomial of another [Cox, Little and
O'Shea 2015, ch. 2, §7, Proposition 6].

**Theorem B-5 (membership).** If $G$ is a Gröbner basis of $I$, then
$f \in I$ if and only if the remainder of $f$ on division by $G$ is $0$
[Cox, Little and O'Shea 2015, ch. 2, §6, Corollary 2].

## Cofactors

**Proposition B-6 (cofactor tracking).** Let the inputs be
$f_1, \dots, f_s$. If Buchberger's algorithm records, for every basis
element $g$, polynomials $c_{g,1}, \dots, c_{g,s}$ with
$g = \sum_k c_{g,k} f_k$, updating them at each S-polynomial and each
reduction step by the same linear combinations it applies to $g$, the
invariant holds throughout; then a division of $f$ by $G$ with remainder
$0$, $f = \sum_g a_g g$, yields
$f = \sum_k \big(\sum_g a_g c_{g,k}\big) f_k$.

*Proof.* The inputs satisfy the invariant with unit vectors. An
S-polynomial and a reduction step form $K[x]$-linear combinations of
elements that satisfy it, and applying the same combinations to their
cofactor vectors preserves it. Substituting the cofactor expressions into
the division identity gives the last equation. $\square$

Cofactors can grow much larger than the basis; tracking is optional, and
is switched on only when a membership certificate is wanted.

**Certificate B-C1 (ideal membership).** Data: $h_1, \dots, h_s$. Check:
$f = \sum_k h_k f_k$. Claim: $f \in \langle f_1, \dots, f_s \rangle$.

**Theorem B-7 (what membership certificates prove).** Let
$f = \sum_k h_k f_k$ hold as polynomials with rational coefficients. Then,
in the `scalar` carrier, for every assignment of finite values to the
variables at which $f_1, \dots, f_s$ all vanish, $f$ vanishes. In
particular, if $f = 1$, the polynomials $f_k$ have no common zero among
finite values (a Nullstellensatz certificate of infeasibility).

*Proof.* Evaluation at finite values is a ring homomorphism into
$\mathbb C$ (Theorem P-2 of the kernel's theory). $\square$

This is how Gröbner bases serve proofs: the algorithm finds the cofactors,
and the kernel checks one polynomial identity.

For QQi, B-2 through B-6 apply unchanged because $\mathbb Q(i)$ is a field
(Definition R-1a). Evaluating a B-C1 identity under its embedding in
$\mathbb C$ proves the same vanishing implication for complex assignments;
in particular, a witness for $1$ proves the absence of a common complex zero.
The library checks this identity exactly. The current kernel polynomial
encoding remains rational-only, so this extension does not supply a kernel
rule or encoding for complex coefficient certificates.
